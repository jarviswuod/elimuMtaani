Hackathon build brief · Track: Elimu

Mwalimu wa Grade 10

Night-before teaching packs for a Grade 10 teacher preparing a strand she was never trained for, with no textbook and no internet in the room. Built online at night, used entirely offline in the classroom, holding no learner data.

What we are actually solving

A curriculum design document tells a teacher the outcome — "the learner should be able to explain X" — and never tells her X. With no textbook delivered, there is nowhere for her to go and learn X. So three problems are stacked inside one brief:

  1. She doesn't know the content. The design states outcomes, not subject matter.
  2. She can't deliver it with what's in the room. The design's suggested activities quietly assume a lab, a library, a projector, or internet research. She has chalk and fifty learners.
  3. She has to defend the plan. Schemes of work get inspected. A plan she can't source is a liability.

The rule that shapes every decision we make today:

She cannot verify our output. Nobody in that room can. A farmer knows if the depot turned him away; a nurse knows a bad history when she reads one. A teacher who was never trained on this strand has no way to detect that we invented a plausible-sounding falsehood — and she will teach it to fifty children.

So provenance matters more than polish. Every line in a pack either traces to the design document with a page number, or is visibly labelled as generated. This is not a disclaimer we bolt on at the end. It is the product.

Scoped to one sub-strand, one lesson. Eight sections, fixed order — and the order is the product, because understanding has to come before delivery.

Architecture in one line

A structured curriculum tree feeds six narrow generators, whose output is a self-contained pack that lives on the device and prints to paper.

Three decisions follow from that, and they are the ones that will save or sink the day:

  • Parse the curriculum into structure — don't retrieve over PDF chunks. The designs are already structured. A parsed tree gives exact, citable nodes; chunked retrieval gives fuzzy matches and citations we can't stand behind. The corpus is a few dozen sub-strands — it's a JSON file, not a vector database.
  • Six small generators, never one mega-prompt. Each has its own contract, its own validation, its own risk profile. A partial failure still ships a usable pack; a monolith fails whole.
  • Offline is a file problem, not a runtime problem. Once downloaded, a pack needs no server, no API, no model. Then offline isn't something we engineered — it's a property we get free.

Member 1 — Curriculum & Data

You own the grounding source. Everything downstream cites you.
1. Hand-enter the corpus. One subject, one strand, three to six sub-strands. Outcomes, inquiry questions and suggested experiences verbatim, each with a real page number. the JSON matches shared/src/curriculum.ts and every node has a page.
2. Idempotent seed loader. npm run seed loads data/curriculum/*.json into Postgres with ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE. You'll run it twenty times today. running it twice in a row changes nothing and errors nowhere.
3. Curriculum read API. The four GET endpoints in shared/src/api.ts. Every object carries its SourceRef — a query that drops doc_page is a bug, not an omission. you can walk subject → strand → sub-strand → detail with curl.
4. Tag assumedResources. For each suggested experience, list what it quietly assumes she has: internet, laboratory, textbook, projector, printer, field trip. This single field is what Member 2's activity translator runs on. every experience has a list, and empty means "needs no translation".
5. Citation formatter. One helper that renders a SourceRef as "KICD CSL Grade 10 Design, p. 24". Used by the UI and the PDF export so a citation never renders two different ways. frontend and export both call it; neither formats a citation itself.

Member 2 — Generation Engine

You own the only code that talks to the model — and the section she can't check.
1. Briefing generator. Teaches the teacher the content. Effort max — this is the one section with no document to fall back on, so it carries the whole verification risk. Scope it hard to the outcome text; include the misconceptions she is likely to hold herself. someone who doesn't know the topic can read it and teach the lesson.
2. Activity translator. Takes a real suggested experience plus its assumedResources and her actual resource level, and rewrites it for her room. Grounded by construction — the input is a real document line. Every material free or a few shillings, plus a no-materials fallback. the learning intent is identical and nothing on the list needs a shop.
3. Board plan generator. One to four chalk panels: what to draw, what to say while drawing it, optional ASCII sketch. The blackboard is our render target — there is no projector. you could stand at a board and follow it without rereading.
4. Questions and rubric generators. Three to eight questions learners really ask, answered, plus the don't-know script. Quick checks and a four-level rubric row per outcome. both return schema-valid output with no missing rubric level.
5. Orchestrator with graceful degradation. Promise.allSettled across the generators; each rejection becomes a warning and a missing section, never a failed request. Your code stamps provenance, never the model — the model does not get to tell us how trustworthy it is. you can break one generator on purpose and still get a usable pack.

Member 3 — Pack API, Cache & Export

You own the artifact leaving the building — and the demo's insurance policy.
1. POST /api/packs end to end. Validate, check cache, load sub-strand, generate, persist, return. one curl produces a complete stored pack.
2. The cache. sha256(sub-strand + context), unique index, DB hit before any API call. The second teacher to ask about a sub-strand pays nothing and waits nothing. an identical repeat request returns instantly with zero API calls, and you can show that.
3. Self-contained HTML export. One file, inline CSS, zero external requests. This is what makes offline free and makes teacher-to-teacher sharing need no infrastructure at all. the file opens correctly from the filesystem with wifi off.
4. PDF export. Server-side render of that same HTML. Two sides of A4 — there is no power at the front of her classroom, so paper is a real output format. it prints legibly on physical A4 without cutting a section in half.
5. Demo pre-warm script. npm run prewarm generates and caches the exact demo path. Then a flaky venue network, a rate limit, or an expired key cannot touch the pitch. the demo path loads from cache with the API key removed entirely.

Member 4 — Prep Flow (frontend)

You own everything she touches. Three taps from open to pack.
1. The picker. Subject → strand → sub-strand. Build it against GET /api/packs/sample from minute one — don't wait for Members 1 and 2. three taps reach a sub-strand on a phone-width screen.
2. Context form. Class size, lesson minutes, language, resource level — all with defaults good enough to skip. Every extra field costs a tap, and she is doing this at 9pm. she can go straight from picker to Build without typing anything.
3. Pack view. All eight sections in the fixed order, phone-shaped, skimmable in four minutes. If it can't be skimmed at 9pm it won't be read at all. the sample pack renders completely, top to bottom, on a phone.
4. Provenance chips. Every section labelled from the design / generated, with the citation line where one exists. This is the visible form of the project's core rule — it is a feature, not a footnote. no section renders without a provenance chip.
5. Honest progress. Generation takes 20–40 seconds. Name what's being built as it lands, section by section. Never a bare spinner, never a blank screen. a first-time user never wonders whether it has hung.

Member 5 — Offline, Print & Share

You own the claim the whole pitch rests on. Your work is the demo's spine.
1. Installable PWA. Service worker precaches the app shell; installs to an Android home screen. No app store, no download, no 40MB APK on her data bundle. it installs on a real phone and opens from the home screen.
2. IndexedDB pack store. Save on generate, read on open. Local first, network as fallback — offline is the expected state in her classroom, not the error case. opening a saved pack makes no network request at all.
3. Airplane-mode proof. The verifiedOffline badge, set once a pack has been opened with no network. And the offline banner reads "your saved packs still work" — never an error. wifi off, hard reload, pack still opens. Rehearse this one.
4. Print stylesheet. Whole pack on two sides of A4. Sections never split across a page break. Provenance survives to paper — a printed claim with no source is exactly the failure we exist to prevent. it prints on real A4 and you'd teach from the printout.
5. Share. Web Share API to push the exported file to WhatsApp or Bluetooth, with a plain download fallback. Zero-infrastructure teacher-to-teacher distribution — sneakernet as a feature. a pack moves from one phone to another with no server involved.

Contracts and merge protocol

  • shared/src/* is frozen. It's the reason five people can work at once. Changing it needs a group decision, and everyone runs npm run build:shared after pulling.
  • Branch per member — m1-curriculum, m2-generation, and so on. Touch only your own directories.
  • Merge continuously, not at the end. Anyone still sitting on a six-hour-old branch at hour five is a merge disaster with a countdown on it.
  • main must always start. If your merge breaks docker compose up, you stop and fix it before anything else.
  • Build against GET /api/packs/sample. Nobody waits for anybody.

The demo, 90 seconds

  1. "It's 8:47pm. Grade 10, period two tomorrow. She has never taught this strand, and there is no textbook."
  2. Three taps: subject, strand, sub-strand.
  3. The pack appears. Scroll the briefing. "This teaches her, not the class. That's the part everyone else skips."
  4. Point at a provenance chip. "This line is from the design, page 24. This one we generated — and we say so, because she has no way to check us."
  5. Turn on airplane mode. Hard reload. Still there.
  6. Print preview. Two sides of A4, because there's no power at the front of the room.
  7. "No child logs in. There is no student table in our schema. Audit it."

Four things we are deliberately not building

  • No learner-facing anything. No child logs in, no names, no submitted work, no photographs of exercise books. It's structural, not a policy promise — and one "share it with students" feature would disqualify us.
  • No vector database or embeddings. A few dozen sub-strands is a JSON file. Chunked retrieval would also break our citations, which are the product.
  • No PDF parser. Automated table extraction on these designs will eat four hours and produce mush. Manual entry of six sub-strands takes under an hour and is flawless. In a one-day build, typing is the correct engineering decision.
  • No on-device model. "No internet in the room" is not "no internet ever". She preps at home on data; the artifact is what goes offline.
